In August 1590, a ship's crew landed on Roanoke Island, off the coast of what is now North Carolina. They found a fort torn down, houses dismantled, and no people.

The man leading the party was John White, governor of the English colony. His own daughter, son-in-law and granddaughter were supposed to be there. The granddaughter, Virginia Dare, had been born in the colony three years earlier. She was the first English child born in the Americas.

## The colony

The 1587 expedition brought about 115 to 117 men, women and children. Soon after they landed, relations with local Native peoples broke down. Supplies were short, and White sailed back to England to get help.

He expected a quick return. Instead, England was in the middle of war with Spain, and ships were taken for the fight against the Spanish Armada. Three years passed.

## What he found

White left an agreement with the colonists: if they had to leave, they were to carve the name of their destination, and if they were in danger, to add a cross. When he returned, he found the word "CROATOAN" carved on a post at the entrance to the settlement, and the letters "CRO" on a tree. There was no cross.

Croatoan was the name of a nearby island, now part of the Outer Banks, and of the Native people living there, who were friendly to the English. White hoped his people had gone there. Bad weather prevented him from searching, his ships turned back, and he never saw them again.

## Theories

- **They moved in with the Croatoan.** This is widely seen as the most plausible, since the carving points to it and the colonists lacked supplies.
- **They moved inland.** A map White drew shows a mark that some researchers think hints at a planned move. Archaeologists working on a site in Bertie County, North Carolina, found English-made objects mixed with Native artifacts, but whether that is the colonists is not established.
- **They were killed.** Later English accounts mention reports of settlers killed by Native peoples, but those accounts are second-hand and unreliable.
- **They tried to sail home and were lost at sea.** There is no evidence for this either.

Many people over the years have claimed to find the answer, including supposed carved stones that were later shown to be hoaxes.

## Why it's still open

The strongest evidence is circumstantial. Archaeologists have found some European artifacts on Hatteras Island, but not conclusive proof of the colonists' fate. Digging is hard on a coast that has been reshaped by storms and rising seas.

*Editorial note: Exact numbers and some details, such as how many colonists there were, vary across sources. Any claim to have "solved" Roanoke should be treated cautiously until archaeologists confirm it.*
