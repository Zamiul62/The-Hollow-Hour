In the 1990s, a German archaeologist climbed a hill in southeastern Turkey that locals had known for generations as just a mound. Earlier surveys had dismissed it as a medieval cemetery. Beneath the soil, Klaus Schmidt found something that did not fit the textbook.

Enormous stone pillars, some shaped like the letter T, stood in circles. They were covered with carved foxes, boars, snakes and birds. And they were about 11,000 years old.

## The date that broke the timeline

Göbekli Tepe dates to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, roughly 9500 BCE, which is thousands of years before pottery, before writing, and before the wheel. It is more than 6,000 years older than Stonehenge. The site, in the Şanlıurfa region, covers about 300 metres across, and the mound is up to 15 metres high in places.

According to the German Archaeological Institute, which runs the excavation, the monumental buildings were in use over a period of more than 1,500 years. The tallest T-shaped pillars reach about 5.5 metres, and each weighs many tonnes.

The old model said people built monuments *after* they settled down and farmed, because that was when they had spare time, food and organization. Göbekli Tepe suggests the reverse might be at least partly true: that gathering to build something shared may have helped push people toward settled life.

## Who built it?

The honest answer is that nobody knows their name, language or what they called themselves. The builders are generally thought to have been hunter-gatherers, or people at the very start of the transition to farming.

That is why calling it a "lost civilization" is a little misleading. This was not a city-state with kings and writing. It was something stranger: a ceremonial centre raised by people who, as far as we can tell, had not yet invented most of the things we use to define a civilization.

## Not just a temple?

For years Göbekli Tepe was described as "the world's first temple", a place that was only visited and never lived in. The excavators now argue for a more nuanced view. Fire installations, food remains from animals and grains, water cisterns, burials and many buildings indicate that people did live there. The DAI now interprets it as a settlement with a strong ritual component.

Some reports also mention grinding stones and flint sickles, which suggest food processing, and perhaps early farming. Whether these show full agriculture is still debated.

## The most haunting detail

At some point, the builders deliberately buried their monuments. The enclosures were filled in, and new ones were built on top or beside them. Why bury something that took so much labour to raise?

Researchers have suggested it could have been part of a ritual cycle, a closing ceremony, or a way of renewing sacred places. Nobody can prove any of these. The site was eventually covered and forgotten, which is part of why it survived so well.

## What we still don't know

Only a fraction of the site has been excavated. Estimates in news reports suggest roughly 10 percent, and the full excavation could take generations. Newly found structures, including three more enclosures, are still being studied. Nearby sites such as Karahan Tepe suggest this was not a single isolated place.

Open questions include:
- What the animals and symbols on the pillars meant.
- Whether they relate to a shared belief system or to clan identities.
- What reason the builders had for burying the enclosures.
- Why the site was abandoned around 8000 BCE.

*Editorial note: Claims that Göbekli Tepe is evidence of a "lost advanced civilization" are speculation. The excavators' interpretation is of a hunter-gatherer society with remarkable monumental building.*
