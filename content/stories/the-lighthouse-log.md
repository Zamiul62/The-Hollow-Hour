*This is a work of fiction written for The Hollow Hour. Names, places and events are invented.*

The relief crew heard the bell before they saw the rock.

It was a clear, cold morning, the first clear one in three weeks, and the sea lay flat and hard as slate. Hesketh, the boatman, cut the engine a half mile out so he could be sure of what he was hearing. Across the water came a single dull note, a pause, and then another. *Clang.* Twenty seconds. *Clang.* Twenty seconds. Not a hand's rhythm, which drifts and tires. The bell was being struck by a machine, with the evenness of a clock.

"Fog bell," said Tom Alder, the new relief keeper, who had come out to Skerrow Rock to take over for the winter. "But there's no fog."

"No," said Hesketh. He restarted the engine. "There's not."


The landing was salt-white and empty. The tower door at the bottom was bolted from *inside*, and it took the three of them, and a crowbar from the boat, to break it. The kitchen was frozen. Ice had formed on the inside of the windows in feathers. The tins in the larder were gone, every last one, stacked empty and rinsed in the sink. The stove was cold, and the coal bin held only dust.

The bell, they discovered, hung in a small iron gallery under the lamp room, and it was struck by a hammer connected to a rod, a cord and a heavy weight that was crawling slowly downward through a hole in the floor. Someone had rigged the weight to the clockwork that normally turned the lamp.

At the winding crank, beside the clockwork, sat Elias Marrow.

He had been dead for at least a day. He sat upright on a crate with one hand frozen to the crank and his face turned toward the northern window, where the sea was a long, empty line. His eyes were open. His other hand held a pencil. On his knee lay the logbook, open to the last page.

Alder took the book and began to read. Outside, the bell went on, and the weight sank another inch.

## The log of Skerrow Rock

**NOVEMBER 2.** *Gillespie, my assistant, was taken ill in the night and went back on the morning boat with a fever and a grudge. The boat will not return before the 24th. Barometer falling. I am alone. Tower in good order. Diaphone horn tested and sounding. Lamp lit at 4:41. All well.*

Marrow had been a keeper for nine years, and he wrote like one. His entries were dry and exact, and for the first week they stayed that way.

**NOVEMBER 5.** *Gale from the south-west all night. Wave action over the landing, green water on the lower gallery. Radio mast carried away at 0215. The set is dead without it. I can neither call out nor be called. This is not unusual. Relief is nineteen days off, the larder is full, and the light does not need a radio.*

**NOVEMBER 8.** *Wind dropped, and fog came in behind it as it does after a blow, warm air over cold water. I could not see the landing from the kitchen door. Sounded the horn at the regulation interval, two blasts every minute. Cannot see the lamp from the foot of the tower. A good night to be a lighthouse.*

The fog, according to the next entries, did not lift. Alder noticed that Marrow stopped recording the weather in numbers and started recording it as a mood. *Thick. Wet. Close.* By the 11th: *The fog is the kind you can taste. It leaves salt on my lips in the lamp room, ninety feet up.*

**NOVEMBER 12.** *Heard the horn answered tonight. Two blasts, from the north, a mile off, perhaps two. A deeper note than ours, two tones together, like a chime whistle. A steamer, I thought, lost in the murk and feeling for the coast. Sounded again. It answered again. I have no way of telling her where she is. If she holds her course she will pass north of the Rock by a good margin. I put out the signal lamp and stood watching until my eyes stung. Nothing.*

**NOVEMBER 13.** *Heard it again at dusk. Same note, same bearing. Same interval, I realized. Exactly twenty seconds after my second blast. Echo, then. In fog the sound can bounce off a bank of colder air and return changed. I have read of it. The deeper tone is simply the horn after a long journey. I feel better for knowing.*

The pencil pressure was lighter in that last line, Alder thought. The pencil of a man who wanted very much to believe what he was writing.

**NOVEMBER 15.** *The fog is nine days old. There has been no moon, no star, no horizon. I have started talking to myself, which I count as a warning, and am putting a stop to it by reading aloud to the lamp instead.*

*Went down to the oil store for lamp oil and found a locked cabinet I had forgotten, with the logs of earlier keepers. Took out the oldest, for company. The entries are from 1911, by a keeper named Joseph Pell. His handwriting is so close to a copperplate I thought it was printed.*

*The pages for the last week of October that year have been cut out with a razor. Neatly. I can see the stubs.*

*Pell's last full entry reads, "Diaphone compressor fouled with damp. Will see to it at first light." I thought no more about it, until I recalled the date. The twenty-ninth of October. It was in the papers my father kept. The same night, the steamer Halcyon Rose struck the Rock in fog, with sixty-three souls aboard, because no horn warned her off.*

*The inquiry blamed the weather. Pell was relieved of duty and never worked again. I found a cutting tucked inside the cover. The Halcyon Rose carried a chime whistle with two notes. A mourner at the inquiry described how survivors on the shore heard it blow, over and over, as she drifted down onto the reef.*

*I have decided not to think about this any more.*

**NOVEMBER 16.** *I thought about it all night.*

*The answer, tonight, did not come twenty seconds after my second blast. It came before my second blast. It came in the gap, with the dead air still hanging, so I was standing at the horn with my hand on the lever and heard the deep two-note whistle from the north, closer than before, and then my own horn, a moment after, as if I were the echo.*

*I did not sleep. I have the lamp room lit. It makes no difference. The fog is thick as wet wool and the beam goes out into it for perhaps a hundred feet before it dies.*

## The knocking

**NOVEMBER 18.** *Compressor failed at 2 a.m. Damp in the air line, as Pell wrote. The horn is dead. I spent six hours with the tools and a bucket of paraffin and got nowhere. The part that has failed would have to be replaced, and I have no spare.*

*Within the hour, the whistle answered the silence. Nearer. I could hear, under it, the thud of engines, and a high thin sound that I took at first for wind in the rigging and then, as it grew, understood was people shouting. A great many voices, a long way off, and none of them urgent. The sound of a crowd waiting for something.*

*I climbed to the bell gallery. I have never rung the fog bell in anger; it is a backup, a hand bell on a rope. I rang it at the regulation interval, and the whistle fell away to the north.*

*Rang it for eleven hours. Until my hands bled. Slept in the gallery on the iron floor, for perhaps forty minutes, until I woke on my side and found the bell silent. The rope had slipped from my fingers.*

*The tower was full of sea air. I heard water dripping, and a long way below me, the sound of the bottom door being knocked on.*

*Three knocks. Polite. A pause. Three more.*

**NOVEMBER 19.** *I did not answer the door. I want that written down. I took the bell rope and rang it until the knocking stopped, and then I went down, one flight at a time, with the lantern held out in front of me.*

*The floor of the passage was wet. Salt water, standing in a thin sheet from the door to the stair. On it were footprints. Many of them, bare and booted, large and small, all leading in from the landing and up toward the kitchen. None going out.*

*They stop at the third step. Every one of them, at the third step, as if the people who made them had stopped to listen and then turned back to the door.*

*I put the bolt across. I fetched my father's old boat anchor and leaned it against the door. Then I went back up to the bell and I rang it.*

**NOVEMBER 20.** *The whistle is under the Rock now. I can hear it through the stone. Beyond the western landing, where the fog breaks briefly with each swell, I saw the lights of a ship. Portholes, in two rows, and the red glow of a funnel lamp. She is moving with the sea, slow and steady, perhaps fifty yards from the rocks.*

*I do not know what I saw. I know what it looked like. It looked like a ship that had been steaming toward this reef for forty years and was surprised, each time, to find it.*

*Every time I ring, she sheers away. Every time I stop, she turns toward the light.*

**NOVEMBER 21.** *I have an idea. The lamp clockwork is a descending weight on a drum, a good one, built to turn a three-ton lens for six hours between windings. If I take the lamp's weight and connect it to the bell hammer by the iron rod from the old storm shutter, it will strike on its own. I can wind it every three hours. It means I can sleep. It also means I can't stop.*

*The light itself will have to go dark. I have thought about this for some time. A ship with a bell to steer by is safer than a ship with a lamp she cannot see.*

*It is done. The bell strikes every twenty seconds. I am so tired I cannot stand. I am going to lay down on the crate for a moment.*

## The last entries

**NOVEMBER 22.** *They knocked again at dusk. Many hands this time, a soft, patient, wooden drumming that I felt through the walls. And a man's voice, a clear educated voice, calling up the stairwell.*

*"Hello the light! We saw your lamp, and then we lost it. Is this Skerrow? Is there a keeper? We've had an awful time of it."*

*I know that voice. I have read it in the inquiry. It is the voice of the Halcyon Rose's first officer, who is quoted at length in the cutting Pell kept, and who spoke with a slight stammer on the letter H.*

*"Hello the light," said the voice again. "Won't you open the door?"*

*It did not stammer.*

**NOVEMBER 23.** *Food is gone. Water is tainted; the cistern has a salt taste that was not there a week ago. I do not think I can wind the weight again. My hands won't close.*

*The bell is striking. They stand at the bottom of the tower and listen. I know they listen because the knocking stops during each stroke. They are not angry. That is what I cannot get past. They are not angry with me, or with Pell. They are only waiting for the bell to stop.*

*Pell was a better man than the inquiry knew. I think that on the night of the twenty-ninth he understood what was in the fog before it struck, and I think he cut the pages out because he could not bear to have written it down.*

*If you are reading this, you are relief, and the fog has lifted. Do not stop the bell. Do not stop it until you have been told by someone who is alive that the fog has gone for good. It will lift. It always lifts.*

*And then it comes back.*

**NOVEMBER 24.** *The boat should be here today. I can hear the bell. I can hear them waiting. I wonder if Gillespie's replacement will be a good man or a clever one, and I realize that it makes no difference, because*

The entry stopped there. The pencil had been laid down in the gutter of the book, and a tiny curl of lead had broken off at the point.

## Alder

Alder read the last line twice. Below him, in the stairwell, something knocked.

It was only a small sound, three knocks with a pause. It might have been anyone. Hesketh was standing right beside him in the gallery, and the third man was outside on the landing, in the sun.

"Tom," said Hesketh softly. "Tom, don't."

"I'm not going down."

"The weight's almost out."

He was right. The great iron weight had sunk nearly to the floor, and the next stroke might be the last. Alder looked at Marrow, upright on his crate with his hand on the crank, and then at the horizon, where a low pearly line was beginning to form between the sea and the sky.

Fog, coming in from the north. Slowly, the way it does after a clear morning.

He did not decide anything. His hands decided for him. He took Marrow's frozen fingers off the crank, one at a time, and laid the man gently on the floor. Then he took hold of the handle and began to wind.

*Clang.* Twenty seconds. *Clang.*

The knocking on the bottom door stopped.

Hesketh stood watching him for a long time. At last he took the log from the desk, and the pencil, and put them into Alder's free hand.

"Write it down," he said. "Somebody has to. Somebody has to tell the next one."

Alder wrote, in a hand not yet steady, with the bell still ringing over his head.

**NOVEMBER 25.** *Relief boat landed at nine. Weather clear, but fog building to the north. Keeper Marrow dead at his post. The bell is sounding. All well.*
