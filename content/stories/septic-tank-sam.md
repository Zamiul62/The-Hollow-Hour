*This article describes a violent crime. It avoids graphic detail, but readers who find that distressing may want to skip it.*

On April 13, 1977, a married couple in central Alberta decided to save a little money.

Charlie and Mavis McLeod needed a pump for their septic system. They knew where a perfectly good one was sitting: in the tank on an old farm they no longer lived on, a short drive from Tofield, about 70 kilometres east of Edmonton. Buying a new one would cost money. Pulling the old one out would cost them an afternoon.

They lifted the lid.

## What was in the tank

Inside the tank was the body of a man. He had been wrapped in a yellow bedsheet and tied with rope before being dropped in, according to reports of the case. A large amount of quicklime, a chemical that speeds the breakdown of remains, had been poured over him. Someone had wanted him to disappear, and had put thought into it.

By the time the McLeods found him he was too decomposed to be recognized. Experts at the time believed he had been there for a long period, with estimates ranging from several months to at least a year. His body had also been burned.

The investigation found that he had been tortured before he died. Reports state that he was shot. We are not going to list the rest, because the details add nothing except shock. What matters is that this was not a random accident or a quiet death. It was a prolonged act of violence, followed by a deliberate attempt to hide the evidence.

Police also concluded that he was most likely killed somewhere else. The tank contained little evidence of the crime itself, which suggests it was only a dumping ground. That raises a question that has never been answered: why this farm? It was abandoned, isolated, and easy to forget. Did the person who used it know the property? Did they happen to find it? Nobody can say.

## The nickname

With no name, investigators gave him one. He became "Septic Tank Sam", and sometimes "Sam Doe" or "Tofield John Doe". Some people who follow unidentified-persons cases have objected to the first nickname, because it ties a man's memory to the worst place his body was left. We use it here because it is how the case is searched for, but we will call him by his real name from this point on.

In 1988, police released a facial reconstruction to try to jog someone's memory. Nobody came forward with an answer. He was buried in an unmarked grave in Edmonton.

For decades, that was where the story sat. A man had been murdered with remarkable cruelty and no one could even tell police who to mourn.

## Why nobody came looking

It is easy to assume that a missing person leaves a loud gap behind them. Often they do not. Investigators eventually suspected that he was not from the area, that he had few close relatives, or that he lived a life that was frequently on the move. Any of those would make it easy for a disappearance to go unreported.

The truth was sadder and more specific.

## A name, forty-four years later

In October 2017, the Alberta RCMP launched a national program to identify unidentified remains using DNA. By 2020, with every older lead exhausted, the RCMP and Alberta's medical examiner's office sent samples to Othram, a Texas laboratory that specializes in recovering usable DNA from old, degraded evidence.

The method is the same kind of genetic genealogy that led to the arrest of the Golden State Killer in 2018. Investigators build a DNA profile, compare it against public genealogy databases, and trace the family tree of the relatives who match until it points to one person.

It worked. Police identified him in January 2021. They announced it publicly on June 30, 2021.

His name was Gordon Edwin Sanderson. His family called him Gordie.

## Who Gordie was

Staff Sgt. Jason Zazulak of the Alberta RCMP major crimes unit described him plainly at the news conference: "Gordie had a hard life."

Sanderson was an Indigenous man born in Manitoba. When he was nine, he was taken from his family during what is known as the Sixties Scoop, a period in which large numbers of Indigenous children in Canada were removed from their families and placed in foster care or adopted. He had been living in Edmonton since the 1970s.

The last time his family heard from him, he was on his way to meet his younger brother Arthur in Calgary. He never arrived.

For 44 years, a family had a hole in it with no explanation, and a man lay in a grave with no name. Sanderson was survived by an older sister and a daughter. His sister was still alive when the identification was made, so she was able to learn what happened to her brother in her lifetime. His age at death is reported as 25 in some accounts and 26 in others; the CBC corrected an early report that had given it as 20.

## What is still unknown

The identification solved the mystery of who he was. It did not solve the murder.

Police said the case became an active homicide investigation once he was identified. As of the sources cited here, nobody has been charged, and the main questions remain open:

- Where was Gordie killed?
- Who was with him between that trip toward Calgary and the day his body was left on the farm?
- Who put him in that tank, and did they know the McLeod property?
- Why was this much effort spent hiding him?

Those who knew Gordie in the mid-1970s, in Edmonton or elsewhere, may now hold the missing pieces simply because they can finally attach a name to a memory. The RCMP continues to ask anyone with information to contact the Alberta RCMP or their local police.

## The small details that stay with you

Greg McLeod, the son of the couple who made the discovery, was 15 at the time. He told CBC's The Fifth Estate that his father never talked about it, and he never knew any details until the identification was announced.

That may be the most unsettling part of the story. A family went to retrieve a pump, opened a lid, and spent the rest of their lives not discussing what they saw. Meanwhile, someone out there knows what happened to Gordie Sanderson. They have had more than four decades to think about it.
