Drive east out of St. Louis, across the Mississippi into Illinois, and you pass a grassy hill rising out of the flat floodplain. It is about 100 feet tall. It is the largest earthen structure built in North America before European contact, and it was shaped by hand, basket by basket.

It is called Monks Mound, a name given by Trappist monks who farmed on it in the early 1800s. The people who built it left no written record. Their city, now known as Cahokia, was once the largest urban center north of Mexico.

## A city that appears almost overnight

Around 1050 CE, the community near the river grew dramatically. Estimates of the peak population range from about 10,000 to 20,000 people, and the site contained at least 120 earthen mounds. Monks Mound alone contains about 6.6 million cubic metres of earth, and was likely topped by a large building. For centuries after, Cahokia was larger than any other American city until Philadelphia overtook it in the late 1700s, according to some historians.

The city was carefully planned. Its plazas were aligned to the cardinal directions, and some scholars see the whole layout as a model of the cosmos. A circular timber monument nicknamed "Woodhenge" appears to have marked the solstices and equinoxes.

## The burial that still shocks

In 1967 to 1971, archaeologists excavated Mound 72, a ridge-shaped mound south of Monks Mound. It held the burials of many people, including individuals laid out with great ceremony, and groups of what appear to be sacrificed victims. It remains one of the most striking finds in North American archaeology, and it shows that Cahokia was no peaceful village.

## Then, it ends

By the 1200s, Cahokia was in trouble. In the thirteenth century, its residents built a wooden stockade of roughly 15,000 logs around the central area, which suggests conflict or fear. By about 1350 the city was largely abandoned.

Why? Several explanations have been tested:

- **Floods.** Sediment cores from lakes near the site indicate that at least two major floods occurred between 1100 and 1400, and the geographer Samuel Munoz found evidence of a catastrophic flood followed by a drop in corn cultivation. It could have wiped out crops and housing.
- **Drought.** A once-popular theory blamed drought, but later research cast doubt on it. Researchers noted that the diverse food base around Cahokia could have been resilient to climate change.
- **Politics and resources.** Some scholars suggest the city became too big, too dependent on a strict social order, and strained by shortages. Disease, warfare and deforestation are also discussed.

No single cause has been proven. The likeliest picture is a combination, with a slow rather than sudden exit.

## The twist: they didn't all vanish

A common version of this story is that the people of Cahokia disappeared. They didn't. Researchers believe they most likely moved away and became part of other Native American communities. A study in the journal American Antiquity, using chemical traces of human waste in lake sediments, argues that people lived in the area again after the city's decline. The population appears to have rebounded by about 1500, peaked again around 1650, and declined by about 1700.

Even the name is borrowed. "Cahokia" comes from a different Native group that lived in the region when French explorers arrived, not from the builders of the mounds.

## What we still don't know

- What the city's own people called it, and what language they spoke.
- How its rulers held power.
- Exactly how the abandonment unfolded, and which descendants moved where.
