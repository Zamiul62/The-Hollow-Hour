On Easter Sunday in 1722, the Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen sighted a small, treeless island in the Pacific, thousands of kilometres from the nearest continent. On it stood hundreds of enormous stone figures staring inland.

Europeans named it Easter Island. Its people called it Rapa Nui. And a question started that has never really gone away: how did such a small, isolated society carve and move so many giants?

## What is certain

The island has around 900 moai, human figures carved from volcanic stone. Many were quarried at Rano Raraku, a volcanic crater where hundreds of statues, finished and unfinished, still lie in various stages of carving. Many were then moved across the island and set on platforms called ahu. They were made by Polynesian settlers who arrived by canoe, probably around the 13th century, according to radiocarbon dating commonly cited.

No outside civilization built them. No lost super-race is needed. This was one of the most remote human settlements on Earth, and its people were skilled sailors, carvers and engineers.

## The collapse story

The popular account goes like this: the islanders cut down their forests to move statues and clear land, the ecosystem failed, the population crashed, and warfare followed. It became a famous parable, popularized by books such as Jared Diamond's *Collapse*, about a society that destroyed itself by exhausting its resources.

## The problem with that story

Recent research has pushed back.

- **The rats.** Polynesian settlers brought rats. Some researchers argue the rats, which can devour palm seeds, played a major role in the loss of the island's forests, rather than people alone.
- **The population.** Estimates of a huge pre-contact population, in the tens of thousands, are disputed. Many researchers now think the number was far smaller, with figures of a few thousand often discussed.
- **The DNA.** A 2024 genome study of ancient islanders reported no evidence of a severe population crash before European contact, according to its authors. The same research suggested the islanders had contact with people from the Americas centuries before Europeans arrived.
- **The statues.** Experiments and Rapa Nui oral tradition both point to the moai having been "walked" upright using ropes, a method that needs people, not many trees.

## The real catastrophe came later

What is widely accepted is that the island's population suffered terribly after contact. Diseases brought by outsiders, and slave raids in the 1860s that carried off a large share of the population, devastated the community. By the late 1870s only about 100 inhabitants remained on the island. Much of the knowledge of the island's writing system, rongorongo, was lost, and it has never been deciphered.

## So what is the mystery?

- How the statues were moved, which is still being tested and debated.
- What *rongorongo* tablets say, if they record language at all.
- How large the population really was, and how it changed.
- What happened to the forests, and how much was caused by people, rats or climate.

The island is not a lost civilization that vanished. Its people are still there, and the Rapa Nui community continues. The haunting part is how a misleading legend about them lasted so long.
