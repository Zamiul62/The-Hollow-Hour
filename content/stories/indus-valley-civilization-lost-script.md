Imagine a city built on a grid, with brick houses, covered drains and a public water system, more than 4,000 years ago. Now imagine that by the time anyone noticed it, nobody alive could remember who built it, what language they spoke, or what they called themselves.

That is the Indus Valley Civilization, also called the Harappan civilization. It is one of the three great early civilizations alongside Mesopotamia and Egypt, and it is by far the least understood.

## A civilization rediscovered by accident

The modern world did not know about it until the 1920s. Excavations at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, in what is now Pakistan, showed that a vast urban culture had flourished across the Indus river plains and beyond, covering a region larger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia.

Its mature phase, from roughly 2600 to 1900 BCE, produced cities with standardized brick sizes, standardized weights, and drainage systems that were remarkable for the time. Its people traded far afield, and Indus seals have been found in Mesopotamia.

## The writing that won't speak

What is missing is just as striking. We have thousands of inscriptions, but we cannot read them. According to Archaeology Magazine, around 5,000 inscriptions written in the Indus script have been found, and no one has managed to work out what the symbols mean. The script appears mostly on seals, tablets and pottery.

There are several reasons it resists decipherment:
- The inscriptions are short. The longest known one has only 34 characters.
- There is no bilingual text, no "Rosetta Stone", to compare it against a known language.
- Nobody knows what language the script writes. Researchers have proposed an early Dravidian language, an early Indo-Aryan language, or a language isolate that disappeared, and the question is still unresolved.
- It isn't even certain that the symbols record a full language, and some scholars have argued they are something closer to a system of signs.

In 2025, the government of Tamil Nadu in India announced a prize of $1 million for anyone who can decipher the script. Claims of decipherment appear regularly, and none has convinced the field.

## What the cities do and don't tell us

Excavated Harappan cities show planning, but they don't show many of the things people expect from an ancient state. Archaeologists have not found an unambiguous royal palace or a clear royal tomb. Weapons are relatively rare. Whether that means a different kind of political system, or that we haven't found the evidence, is debated.

This is the heart of the mystery. We know how these people lived, but not how they were ruled, what they believed, or what stories they told.

## How did it end?

Older explanations blamed invasion. Most scholars no longer accept that, and early claims of a massacre at Mohenjo-daro are not supported by modern analysis.

A 2025 study in the journal Communications Earth & Environment offers a different picture. Researchers reconstructed river conditions and found that the region went through four periods of intense drought between 4,400 and 3,400 years ago. The authors conclude the civilization did not collapse from a single event, but experienced a prolonged and uneven decline in which repeated droughts became a significant contributing factor. After about 4,500 years ago, settlements shifted toward the Indus river.

In other words, the cities probably did not vanish overnight. People adapted, moved and reorganized. Around 3,900 years ago, the large urban centres began to fragment, and the cities faded while rural life continued.

## What remains unknown

- What the script says, and what language it records.
- How the civilization was governed.
- Why a culture so skilled at city-building left so little we can read.
- Exactly how drought, shifting rivers and changes in trade combined to end the cities.
